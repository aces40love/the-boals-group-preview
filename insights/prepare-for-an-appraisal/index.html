How to prepare for a commercial real estate appraisal

A practical checklist for sharing the facts and records that help an appraisal begin with a clearer, more efficient scope.

Have an assignment?

Start with basic assignment facts. Do not send confidential reports or sensitive personal information through an ordinary website or email until a secure transfer method is confirmed.

1. Start with the decision, not the document pile

The most helpful first step is explaining why the appraisal is needed. Is it for a loan, acquisition, estate, property-tax matter, legal dispute, internal planning, or public acquisition? Who will rely on it? Is the relevant value current or tied to a historical or future date?

Those answers shape what documents matter, how the property is researched, and what the report must communicate. A concise explanation of the assignment is more useful than sending every available file without context.

2. Core property records

When available, a current survey, legal description, deed, title information, parcel identifiers, site plans, building plans, and zoning materials can help establish what is being appraised. For land or a partial acquisition, boundaries, easements, access, utilities, and development approvals may be especially important.

Common starting documents
  • Survey and legal description
  • Deed or ownership information
  • Parcel identification numbers
  • Site and building plans
  • Zoning or entitlement documents
  • Environmental or engineering reports, when relevant

3. Records for income-producing property

For leased or operated property, the rent roll, leases and amendments, expense history, current budget, capital expenditures, and details of owner-paid versus tenant-paid costs may be relevant. Provide the actual records available rather than a cleaned-up summary that removes unusual items; questions can be addressed during analysis.

If the property is owner-occupied, identify any space occupied by related parties and any leases or internal rent arrangements. The appraiser can then consider what information is relevant to the real estate rather than the business operating within it.

4. Proposed construction and development

A proposed project may require plans, specifications, construction budget, development schedule, approvals, pre-leasing or presales, and information about phasing. Make clear what is approved, what is proposed, and what remains contingent.

Changes to plans, cost, timing, or approvals can affect the assignment. Flag them early and keep the appraiser informed if they change while the work is in progress.

5. Inspection access and property contacts

Identify the person who can provide access and answer factual questions about the property. If there are multiple buildings, secure areas, tenants, operational constraints, or safety requirements, mention them before scheduling.

An inspection is one source of property information, not a substitute for specialized engineering, environmental, survey, or building-condition work. If a separate report identifies a material condition, provide it when relevant to the assignment.

6. What not to send through an ordinary website form

Do not upload confidential reports, tax identifiers, sensitive personal information, protected legal materials, or large document sets through an unconfirmed web channel. Begin with basic assignment facts. A secure transfer method can be arranged when documents are needed.

There is also no need to provide a “target value.” The appraisal question and intended use should guide the work, not a predetermined result.

7. A concise pre-request checklist

  • Property address, county, and type
  • Client and intended users
  • Intended use or decision
  • Property interest and relevant date, if known
  • Known deadline
  • Property contact and access constraints
  • List of available documents

You do not need to resolve every technical term before contacting an appraiser. The purpose of the first conversation is to identify the right questions.

A note on scope

This is general information, not an appraisal, legal opinion, tax opinion, or advice for a specific property. Assignment requirements depend on the facts and intended use.
